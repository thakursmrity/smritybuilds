Winning back the daily commuter

01 · Goal

Uber is mature, so the goal is retention

Before picking users or ideas, pick the goal. Uber is a dominant player in most cities, with Ola and Rapido competing hard on price and availability. Chasing brand new riders is the expensive path. Keeping the riders it already has is the better use of effort.

Why not growth

Awareness is already high in core cities. New riders cost more to win than existing riders cost to keep.

Why not revenue

Revenue is the outcome, not the lever. It follows from riders staying active, and chasing it directly pushes toward fare and fee changes that hurt trust.

Why retention

Rides per rider is the clearest health signal of a habit product. When it slips, a rival app is usually taking the ride.

Keep existing riders in the habit of booking Uber, starting with the riders showing the first signs of leaving, without hurting driver supply or driver earnings.

Scope

Core city rides: cabs and autos. Outstation trips and rentals are left out, because they follow different behavior and a different business.

In an interview, ask the interviewer whether the goal is growth, revenue or retention. If they leave it open, state your choice and the reason, then move on.

02 · Segments

Pick the lens, then stack a second one

A segment is a group of riders who share a situation. Several lenses can slice the same riders, and the goal decides which one is most useful.

LensExample groupsFit for a retention goal
BehaviorHeavy, medium, light ridersUseful, but a heavy rider is not necessarily at risk
PersonaStudents, professionals, touristsDescribes who they are, not whether they are leaving
Use caseDaily commute, airport, travel destinations, in-city ridesGood for shaping the solution
Loyalty stageActive, drifting, lapsedDirectly shows who is slipping away

Loyalty stage wins for a retention goal because it separates riders by their direction of travel, not just where they are today.

Active

Still riding as usual

Healthy. Protect, do not disturb.

Drifting · chosen

Riding less than before

Still here, but slipping. The last point where a fix is cheap.

Lapsed

Gone to another option

Costly to win back, and the reason they left is hard to find.

[Loyalty stage] + [Use case]

Chosen: drifting daily commuters in metro cities

  • Early signal: they have not left yet, so the fix is still cheap
  • High frequency: a small improvement repeats every working day and compounds
  • Real competition: this is exactly the group Ola, Rapido or a personal vehicle can capture
  • Visible in data: a drop from 4 or 5 weekly rides to 2 or 3 is easy to detect and target
03 · Pain points

Why the commuter rides less

Mapped across the commute: opening the app, price, waiting, the ride, and being recognized as a regular. The ride itself is mostly fine for this group, so the friction sits around it.

Price

Price changes every day

The same route costs different amounts at different times. A commuter plans their month around it.

Waiting

Long waits and cancellations

A driver cancels after accepting, and the wait starts again. Being late for work is a sharp trigger.

Recognition

No reward for loyalty

Someone who rides five times a week gets the same treatment as someone who rides once.

Also noticed, set aside

Inconsistent car condition and driver quality. Real, but it affects all riders equally and is not specific to why commuters drift.

04 · Prioritization

Waiting and cancellations come out on top

Three lenses, each scored 1 to 5. Impact: how much the pain pushes a commuter away. Frequency: how often they run into it. Feasibility: how much Uber can actually fix, where 5 is easiest. Frequency matters because a small, constant annoyance can hurt a daily habit more than a rare, large one.

Waiting and cancellations

High impact, frequent in dense cities, and fixable with product and matching changes without touching the pricing engine.

Price inconsistency

Hurts as much and happens daily, but it is tied to the whole marketplace pricing model. Changing it for one group is hard and risky.

No loyalty recognition

Easy to build, but a slow erosion, not the moment someone switches. A good later add-on.

Impact alone points to price. Adding feasibility is what moves waiting to the top. Pick the problem Uber can actually solve.

Scores are hypotheses. To validate them: cancellation rate after driver acceptance by hour, and time from booking to pickup for commute-hour rides.

05 · Problem statement

One problem worth solving

Problem · Waiting and cancellations

For daily commuters in metro cities who are starting to ride Uber less, long waits and driver cancellations after acceptance make Uber feel unreliable on the mornings that matter most, so they try other options. This lowers rides per rider.

Insight: a commuter is not buying a ride. They are buying arriving on time. One cancelled morning costs more trust than ten smooth ones earn.

06 · Solutions

Three solutions, from quick win to long bet

Ideas came from two angles: get a driver to the rider faster, and stop a driver from dropping a ride once accepted. They were then merged and scored.

Solution 1 · Quick win

Cancellation penalty

A driver who cancels after accepting loses matching priority for a short window. Fair exceptions apply: wrong pickup pin, rider no-show, safety concern.

Effort: days to a few weeks. Mostly rules and thresholds.

Solution 2 · Medium bet

Predictive pre-positioning

Uber already knows where and when commuters book. It nudges drivers toward those zones just before the usual window, so supply is there before demand shows up.

Effort: one to three months. A forecasting model plus a driver app nudge.

Solution 3 · Long bet

Recurring commute plan

Set a ride once: same time, same route, five days a week, like a calendar entry. Uber locks in a driver, and the predictable demand can justify a steadier price.

Effort: four to six months or more. Scheduling, pricing, driver incentives, new UI.

How the recurring plan works

Set it once

Pick the days, pickup time and route. It sits like a calendar entry.

Driver locked in

A driver is assigned ahead of time, so the morning is no longer a gamble.

Steadier price

Because demand is committed, the price can be more stable. A side benefit, not the main fix.

Edit or pause

Skip a day or pause the plan. Repeated last minute cancels trigger a small fee.

Idea sources: the pre-positioning model and the pre-booking template came from reading demand patterns. The calendar style plan with a steadier price was the bigger swing. Uber already offers scheduled rides, so the new part is the recurring template and the guaranteed driver.

07 · Trade-offs

What each choice costs

A trade-off is a benefit given up on purpose, decided at the moment of choosing a solution. A risk is something that could go wrong that nobody chose. They look alike, so they are kept apart.

Cancellation penalty

Gain: riders can trust an accepted ride.

Give up: some driver goodwill and flexibility.

Pre-positioning

Gain: shorter waits in commute zones.

Give up: some supply elsewhere, and engineering time on a model.

Recurring plan

Gain: predictability and stickiness.

Give up: some pricing flexibility, since surge is Uber's usual lever.

Sequence

Cheap and fast first, expensive and structural last. Each step earns the right to the next.

Why not only the recurring plan

It is the strongest idea but the slowest. Commuters are drifting now, so quick fixes buy time while the long bet is built.

08 · Metrics and guardrails

How we know it worked

North star for all three: rides per drifting commuter per week, test group versus a control group. A control group keeps rides that merely moved from being mistaken for rides that were won back.

SolutionSuccess metricTarget (illustrative)Guardrail
Cancellation penaltyDriver-initiated cancellation rate after acceptance, tracked weekly20 to 30% relative drop in the first monthDriver appeal rate on penalties, driver active hours and churn
Pre-positioningAverage estimated wait for commute-hour bookings in targeted zones15 to 20% drop in the first one to two monthsSurge accuracy and frequency in non-commute hours staying stable
Recurring planAdoption (plans created), repeat use after week two, and fulfillment rate of on-time pickupsSet from beta resultsDriver supply for non-recurring riders in commute hours, and rider cancellation rate on recurring bookings

Why fulfillment rate matters

If Uber promises a guaranteed driver and then misses, that is worse than never offering the feature. Reliability is the whole point.

A two-sided guardrail

Riders who set up a plan and then cancel often waste reserved driver supply. A free cancel or two, then a small fee or a prompt to edit or pause the plan, protects drivers.

09 · Risks

What could go wrong

Every risk is paired with a guardrail metric, which is its early warning. Risks come after success metrics because a guardrail only makes sense next to the thing it protects.

A penalty solves one side's problem and can quietly break the other. In a marketplace, every guardrail should watch the side you did not design for.

10 · Rollout

Cheap fixes go wide, risky ones start small

Phase 1 · Weeks 1 to 3

Test the penalty

One or two cities with the highest cancellation rates. Half the drivers see the penalty, half do not.

Continue if cancellations drop and driver complaints stay flat. Then roll out nationally.

Phase 2 · Months 2 to 4

Pilot pre-positioning

A handful of high-commute zones in one or two cities, compared with similar zones without it.

Continue if waits fall and surge elsewhere is untouched. Then expand city by city.

Phase 3 · Months 5 to 6+

Invite-only beta

A small group of known high-frequency commuters already booking the same route most weekdays.

Continue if fulfillment is high and supply for other riders holds. Then open it up broadly.

Takeaways

What this case taught

Name the product type first

A marketplace has two sides. Every idea needs a check against both.

Match the goal to the stage

A mature leader should defend its base before chasing new users.

Stack segment lenses

Loyalty stage found who is leaving. Use case found why. Together they gave a sharp group to solve for.

Feasibility changes the answer

The biggest pain was not the one Uber could fix. Scoring control separately made that clear.

Ship the quick win now, build the model next, and place the long bet only after the earlier steps prove the drivers and riders can both live with it.
